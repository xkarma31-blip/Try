The classroom smelled of chalk and old wood, the kind of room that had seen generations of
students and remembered none of them kindly. Kyouko Kurahashi stood at the desk by the window,
where a boy with a black ponytail tied by a pink ribbon sat alone amid the quiet.

"I-It's been a while, Natsume-kun," she said, and her voice caught on the old familiarity. "Do
you still remember me? I'm Kurahashi Kyouko, from the Kurahashi family."

Natsume looked up. His smile was the sort of thing that didn't belong on a boy's face—too
soft, too practiced—and his eyes held none of the recognition Kyouko clearly hoped for.

"Kurahashi. Right," he said, cool as the academy's polished floors. "Is there something you
need from me?"

She tried again, about a childhood, about a promise. He didn't recall it. Or chose not to.
Either way, the wall went up, and Kyouko walked away with the distinct feeling of having been
measured and found irrelevant.

Outside, the new school building loomed like a temple that had swallowed a corporate tower—
granite walls, scarlet window frames, the whole contradiction of modern Onmyoudou.

"Huh, here?" Harutora Tsuchimikado squinted up at it. "I still feel like an outsider."

"Prepare your heart," Touji said, hands in his pockets.

Harutora launched into one of his usual speeches—prepared, probably, definitely correcting
himself halfway through—and Touji, as ever, let him walk straight into the realization that
the name Tsuchimikado was less a blessing than a target. By the entrance, the stone komainu
spoke, recorded their auras, and dropped the first odd note of the day: a shikigami was already
registered to Harutora. He didn't have one. He said so, loudly. The komainu did not argue. It
simply sent them upstairs.

The principal's office was nothing like the corridors—warm, book-lined, faintly of tea.
Kurahashi Miyo, gray and upright in a brown kimono, regarded them over the top of her glasses
and called them, without blinking, Natsume's Hishamaru and Kakugyouki. She admitted, with
disarming frankness, that she'd let them in as a favor tied to a certain summer incident she
wouldn't name, and warned them that Natsume was hunted—by people who worshipped Yakou as a god.
Then she smiled, nostalgic, and said the great magician had been terrible at shogi.

Harutora stared. "You met Yakou?"

"I was a child," she said, as if that explained everything, which it didn't, and somehow did.

Ohtomo Jin arrived limping on a wooden leg that looked stolen from a pirate, speaking in a
Kansai lilt about his prestige, and explained that Hishamaru and Kakugyouki had been Yakou's
two retainers. He herded them toward class, promising tips on dodging the principal's ever-present
shikigami, and Touji, unimpressed, muttered that now he understood which Kurahashi they were
dealing with.

The classroom fell silent when they entered. Every eye turned. And then Natsume stood—black
uniform, pink ribbon, the heir who had, half a year late, acquired a shikigami—and said, in a
voice that brooked no argument, that Harutora was his, that the Tsuchimikado name granted no
privilege he hadn't earned, and that Kyouko could take her doubts elsewhere.

Harutora, thoroughly claimed and thoroughly overwhelmed, managed a weak, "Yes, Natsume."

Touji, beside him, said nothing. He didn't need to. The look on Harutora's face said it all:
he was going to get eaten alive, and they both knew it.
